Red flags warranting scrutiny

A record-by-record reading of 1,158 e-GP construction tenders — 645 awards worth ৳3,723.7 crore — surfaces four patterns an auditor should explain: value concentrated in a single firm, one in five contracts signed on the last legal day, competition that vanishes at evaluation, and the same contractor–office pairs recurring again and again.

e-GP Watch · data desk Findings verified 2026-08-22 Source: official e-GP records (Procurement_Database.json)
How to read this. Everything below is drawn from the public e-GP record. Entities and officials are named only because the government's own published data names them as the authorising officer, procuring entity, or awarded supplier. Nothing here is an allegation. Concentration, speed, and repetition are red flags that warrant scrutiny — they have innocent explanations as often as troubling ones. The purpose is to identify where to look next, not to reach a verdict. Read the limitations before quoting any single number.

01The headline

Across 1,158 tenders published by six development authorities and related bodies, 645 resulted in a recorded award. Those awards went to 308 contractors once the spelling variants of the same firm are merged (310 distinct names as filed), authorised by 73 named officials across 11 organisational units.

02Value is extraordinarily concentrated

Of ৳3,723.7 crore in total awards, ৳1,068 crore — 28.7% — went to a single contractor, Spectra Engineers Ltd., across just four tenders. The concentration index (HHI ≈ 927) sits at the edge of "moderately concentrated"; the top ten contractors together hold 52.7% of all value.

03The 28-day cliff

Bangladesh's Public Procurement Rules (e-PG3A, ITT clause 67.2) set a maximum of 28 days between the Notification of Award and contract signing. The distribution of actual delays does not look like a natural process bounded by a deadline — it looks like one engineered to the deadline.

130 contracts — one in five of all awards — were signed on precisely day 28, the last legal day, after which the count falls to zero and stays there. A hard spike at a regulatory ceiling followed by a cliff is the signature of a process managed to the rule rather than by the work.

Fifty-two contracts exceed 28 days — a separate compliance question — and forty-four exceed 60 days. But the day-28 spike, standing alone against empty days on either side of it, is the pattern most worth explaining.

The first number counts genuinely uncontested lots, which large specialist works can legitimately be. The last two are the ones that warrant scrutiny: cases where a field of rivals showed up and was then disqualified down to a single survivor. Mass disqualification is lawful and sometimes correct — but 53 instances is a population an oversight body would want to sample and review, starting with the evaluation reports that recorded why each rival was ruled non-responsive.

Definitional note: this analysis treats 149 / 25.2% as its primary single-responsive rate (it excludes genuinely single-bidder lots); the broader 201 / 34.0% counts every tender with one responsive bidder. Always state which definition a quoted figure uses.

One detail warrants particular scrutiny: Spectra Engineers Ltd. received near-exclusive awards from three different CDA officers — Rajib Das (99%), Asad Bin Anwar (94%), and Kazi Hasan Bin Shams (86%). A single firm dominating one officer's book can be a specialism; the same firm dominating three officers' books within one authority is a firm-and-authority pattern worth explaining. On the ৳881-crore contract, the procuring-entity field itself reads "Project Director(Rajib Das)" — the authorising officer's name embedded in the entity label.

As a control, the absence of this red flag is visible too: Mohammad Muzaffar Uddin (RAJUK) authorised 43 tenders spread across 33 different suppliers — a genuinely broad book.

07Limitations — read before quoting

  1. Association is not causation, and a red flag is not a finding of wrongdoing. Every pattern here has lawful explanations. This document identifies where scrutiny is warranted; it does not conclude that scrutiny would find misconduct.
  2. Name de-duplication is imperfect. Contractor and officer names are free text. Normalisation merges obvious variants but cannot catch every misspelling, transliteration, or genuinely distinct firm sharing a name — so "distinct entity" counts are approximate.
  3. The estimated-cost field is empty throughout the dataset, so this analysis cannot test whether winning prices sat suspiciously close to the engineer's estimate — the most direct test of price rigging. That requires the package-level APP estimates and is deferred.
  4. The single-responsive rate is definition-sensitive (34.0% vs 25.2% vs the 53 mass-disqualification cases). Always state which definition a quoted figure uses.
  5. Coverage. The dataset is what was scrapeable from the public portal at collection time; it is not guaranteed to be every tender these authorities ran, and status fields reflect the portal's state on that date.
  6. Timing precision. Award-to-signing delays come from the two published dates; a data-entry convention that standardises signing dates would itself produce a day-28 spike — one more benign explanation the cliff analysis cannot rule out.

08What would resolve the open questions

  • Pull the APP package estimates for the concentrated contracts and compare winning bid to engineer's estimate — the price-rigging test this dataset can't run. The scraper fetch_app_estimates.py in this repo is built for exactly that once portal access is available.
  • Request the evaluation reports for the 53 mass-disqualification tenders — the documented reasons each rival was ruled non-responsive.
  • Review the file trail on the four highest-frequency repeat pairings and the four single-firm officers above.
  • Ask the CDA to explain Spectra's 28.7% share and its near-exclusive awards from three separate officers.
